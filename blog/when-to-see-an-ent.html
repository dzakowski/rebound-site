When to see an ENT for rebound congestion

Plenty of people quit on their own. Some should not try to. Here is how to tell which group you are in, and what the appointment actually involves.

By the Rebound.org editorial team Published November 21, 2026 3 min read 6 sources

When should you see an ENT?

See an ENT (otolaryngologist) if you have used a decongestant spray daily for months or years, have tried and failed to quit more than once, or are still congested 2–3 weeks after stopping. Go sooner for frequent nosebleeds, loss of smell or suspected sleep apnea. Vision changes, facial swelling or a severe headache alongside sinus symptoms need emergency care.

Signs it is time

  • Daily decongestant spray use for months or years
  • More than one failed attempt to quit
  • Congestion that has not improved 2–3 weeks after stopping
  • Recurrent nosebleeds
  • Reduced or lost sense of smell
  • Loud snoring, pauses in breathing at night or other signs of sleep apnea
  • Panic or anxiety around breathing that is affecting daily life

Go to urgent or emergency care for

Severe sinus pain with a fever (urgent care), or vision changes, swelling around the eyes or face, or a severe headache (emergency department). These are not rebound congestion and should not wait for an appointment.

What happens at the visit

  1. History. How long you have used the spray, how often, and what happens when you stop. Bring the bottles. Be honest about the numbers; the doctor has heard bigger ones. Rebound congestion is diagnosed mainly from this conversation. There is no blood test for it, and ENT surgeons surveyed in Canada reported relying chiefly on the history (national survey, 2019).
  2. A look inside the nose with a light and a small speculum. Rebound congestion typically shows a red, swollen lining.
  3. Nasal endoscopy. A thin scope passed through the nostril after a numbing spray. It takes a few minutes and shows the turbinates, the septum and any polyps.
  4. CT scan of the sinuses, only if chronic sinusitis, polyps or a structural problem is suspected.
  5. Allergy testing, if allergies may be what drove the spray use in the first place.

Part of the purpose is to rule other things out. Rebound congestion often sits on top of an untreated problem such as allergic rhinitis, a deviated septum or enlarged turbinates (StatPearls).

What an ENT can offer that the pharmacy cannot

  • A prescription combination spray (azelastine plus fluticasone).
  • A short course of oral steroids for severe cases, alongside a steroid nasal spray. The evidence for this is limited to case series rather than randomized trials, so it is a judgment call for your doctor.
  • A structured weaning plan with follow-up, often built around the one-nostril method.
  • Allergy treatment, including immunotherapy.
  • Assessment for structural causes and, when they are found, a conversation about surgery.

Questions worth asking

  • Is my septum deviated?
  • Are my turbinates enlarged?
  • Do I have polyps?
  • Could allergies be driving this?
  • How long would you expect it to take me to get off the spray?
  • If a steroid spray does not work, what is plan B?

Not ready for a specialist?

Start with your primary care doctor or a pharmacist. If you have used the spray for less than about six months and are motivated, a self-directed quit with a steroid spray and saline is reasonable; the playbook lays out three methods. If you have used it for years, have failed repeatedly, or suspect sleep apnea, book the ENT.

Common questions

Can an ENT tell if I have rebound congestion?

Usually yes. The diagnosis is made mainly from your history of spray use and what happens when you stop, supported by an examination of the nose. There is no blood test for rebound congestion.

Do I need to see an ENT to quit Afrin?

Not always. People who have used a decongestant spray for weeks to a few months often quit on their own with a steroid nasal spray and saline. See an ENT if you have used it for years, have failed more than once, or are still congested 2–3 weeks after stopping.

What kind of doctor treats nasal spray addiction?

An otolaryngologist (ENT) or an allergist. A primary care doctor or pharmacist can start treatment and refer you if needed.
